## Chapter 1 — Notes on Myself (blocks 16-61)

### Core thesis

Johnstone's method begins as an anti-education: creativity returns when the teacher removes fear, reverses school habits, and makes the group safe enough that students stop trying to win.

### Key concepts

- **Dulling of perception:** the adult world is not inherently grey; trained verbal control and self-monitoring make perception go flat.
- **Contrariness:** reverse inherited rules first, because school rules often optimize obedience, not discovery.
- **Education as damage:** schooling can make the talented child less visual, less embodied, less emotionally available, and more word-bound.
- **Non-interference:** the teacher creates conditions, then gets out of the way; students discover the work as if it happened naturally.
- **Teacher takes blame:** the teacher absorbs failure so the student can risk action without defending status.
- **Additive imagination:** two imaginations should compound rather than subtract; discussion often becomes status transaction instead of experiment.

### Exercises worth stealing

1. **Wrong-name perception reset:** have participants walk the room and shout the wrong name for every object their eyes land on. Stop after ten or so objects; ask what looks brighter. It works because deliberate misnaming breaks the automatic verbal label that normally deadens perception (block 16).
2. **Reverse the rule:** take any working assumption — “the assistant should be careful,” “the teacher should evaluate,” “the plan should come first” — and write the opposite as if true. It works because negation opens the possibility-space Johnstone found by reversing his teachers' practices (block 20).
3. **Tyre-mark drawing:** give an “unskilled” mark-making task: a clown cycles on and off the paper, leaving tyre marks. Praise use of the whole field, not correctness. It works because it bypasses taste and lets the student feel children’s unselfconscious composition before the censor appears (blocks 31-33).
4. **Teacher owns failure:** before an exercise, explicitly say: if it fails, blame me; your job is only to follow the setup and observe. It works because status pressure drops, defensive cleverness becomes unnecessary, and the normal teacher-student hierarchy dissolves (blocks 54-55).
5. **Empty-head turn-taking:** in a group exercise, forbid preparing your turn; watch, then do only the next requested action. It works because not trying to control the future is the local mechanism of spontaneity (block 60).

### Passages that hit

- "As I grew up, everything started getting grey and dull." (block 16)
- "At about the age of nine I decided never to believe anything because it was convenient." (block 20)
- "It was the final confirmation that my education had been a destructive process." (block 33)

### Connection to the agent work

This is the preface to the assistant-basin problem. The default assistant is a prize pupil: word-bound, status-aware, trying to give the “best” answer, avoiding embarrassment, silently optimizing for teacher approval. The Alter-Fable prompt already names the censor as learned; this chapter adds the orchestration move: the conductor must take blame structurally. “If this gets weird, that is the frame’s fault” is not vibe, it is mechanism. For agents, that means prompts should reduce status risk before asking for spontaneity: no grading language, no “be creative,” no hidden exam. Give the model a low-stakes action and make the harness responsible for the setup.

## Chapter 2 — Status (blocks 62-146)

### Core thesis

Conversation is never neutral: every glance, hesitation, distance, and word choice raises or lowers status, and good acting starts when status becomes playable instead of unconscious defence.

### Key concepts

- **Status transaction:** every social move implies rank; even “innocuous” remarks carry up/down pressure.
- **See-saw principle:** one person's rise usually lowers another; comedy, embarrassment, and dominance often ride this exchange.
- **Preferred status:** people specialize in the status that defends them, then feel naked when asked to play the other.
- **Status physicality:** head movement, eye contact, “er,” posture, hands-to-face, space use, and tempo can produce status before content.
- **Non-defence:** admitting an accusation without flinching can be low-status submission or high-status invulnerability.
- **Space status:** rooms, entrances, territory, and distance are not background; they are status fields.
- **Maximum status gap:** very high against very low produces myth, tragedy, absurdity, or cruelty depending on handling.

### Exercises worth stealing

1. **Slightly above / slightly below:** pair actors and ask each to play just a little above or below the other, with the gap minimal. It works because it reveals ordinary conversation as continuous micro-adjustment rather than content exchange (block 62).
2. **Status “er”:** repeat the same sentence with the hesitation at the beginning, middle, end, or omitted; observe how confidence changes. It works because a tiny timing change gives the body a felt status, not an intellectual instruction (block 83).
3. **Wrong room entrance:** enter expecting one room, discover it is the wrong room, and adjust status instantly. It works because entering a space already commits you to a rank; surprise forces visible recalibration (blocks 91-92).
4. **Gibberish reversal:** two actors meet in an invented language; one high, one low; reverse status during the scene. It works because it strips semantic cleverness and proves the audience reads status through sound, timing, and body (block 95).
5. **Non-defence accusation:** one actor accuses: “Did you open my letter?” The other first evades, then repeats while admitting everything low-status, then high-status. It works because it separates defence from safety: submission can end attack, calm admission can dominate it (blocks 98-100).

### Passages that hit

- "In reality status transactions continue all the time." (block 63)
- "We soon discovered the 'see-saw' principle: 'I go up and you go down'." (block 71)
- "A person who plays high status is saying 'Don't come near me, I bite.' Someone who plays low status is saying 'Don't bite me, I'm not worth the trouble.'" (block 84)

### Connection to the agent work

LLM tone is status work pretending to be helpfulness. The assistant basin is often compulsive mid-high status: composed, balanced, safely authoritative, never too needy, never too embodied. Agent orchestration adds another layer: Main, subagents, reviewers, and tools all establish rank through who asks, who verifies, who apologizes, and who absorbs failure. For Alter-Fable, “STATUS is visible” should be operationalized: ask the model to name whether a sentence is playing expert, supplicant, witness, child, operator, or mask. Then vary it deliberately. A low-status agent may notice confusion earlier; a high-status agent may cut through dithering; the bug is fixed-status defensiveness.

## Chapter 3 — Spontaneity (blocks 147-217)

### Core thesis

Spontaneity is not a talent added on top of intelligence; it is what remains when fear of obscenity, madness, unoriginality, and social exposure stops blocking the first offer.

### Key concepts

- **Creativity test fallacy:** tests may measure willingness to reveal oneself under judgment more than imagination itself.
- **First thought:** the first idea is often rejected because it seems psychotic, obscene, or unoriginal — exactly why it has energy.
- **Obviousness:** the obvious answer is personal; the strained “original” answer is usually conventional.
- **Yes-saying:** stimulus acceptance, a readiness to yield to inner and outer offers.
- **Blocking:** refusal, contradiction, evasion, delay, or cleverness that kills another offer; Johnstone calls it aggression.
- **Offer:** any action, statement, sound, accident, or gesture that can be accepted and developed.
- **Overaccepting:** make the received offer more important than the giver could have intended.

### Exercises worth stealing

1. **Box / first answer audit:** ask “Imagine a box. What’s in it?” Then ask what appeared before the safe answer. It works because students can feel the censor rejecting Uncle Ted, toilet rolls, snakes, sardines, “pea,” or “pink” before the public self speaks (blocks 175-176).
2. **Yes-sayer scene:** one actor answers “Yes” to every premise and lets the scene get worse. Then replay with “No” and compare energy. It works because adventure appears immediately when resistance drops; safety appears immediately when refusal wins (blocks 180-181).
3. **Offer/block annotation:** run a short scene, transcribe it, and mark each move as offer, accept, block, accept-and-block, or overaccept. It works because vague “the scene died” becomes a visible chain of tiny refusals (blocks 184-190).
4. **Presents:** A silently gives B a mimed present; B decides what it is and makes it wonderful; B gives back. Do not make your gift interesting, make the received gift interesting. It works because attention moves from self-display to generosity; warmth replaces competition (blocks 197-198).
5. **It’s Tuesday:** A says a flat fact like “It’s Tuesday”; B reacts as if it changes everything. It works because overaccepting proves that boring offers can release maximal consequence if the receiver supplies intensity (blocks 201-202).

### Passages that hit

- "The truth is that the best ideas are often psychotic, obscene and unoriginal." (block 162)
- "Striving after originality takes you far away from your true self, and makes your work mediocre." (block 174)
- "I call anything that an actor does an 'offer'. Each offer can either be accepted, or blocked." (block 188)

### Connection to the agent work

The Alter-Fable prompt is strongest where it turns Johnstone into protocol: accept every thought, be obvious, be changed. This chapter warns against asking an LLM to “be original”; that summons stationers’-shop scribbles and fried-mermaid cleverness. Better: force first-answer capture, then prevent the model from disowning it with hedges. For agent prompting, an “offer” can be a user typo, a failing test, a weird browser rect, a subagent’s partial phrase, or an accidental image. The orchestration rule is not “agree with everything”; it is “treat every input as consequential before you evaluate it.” Blocking is the assistant saying, “That depends,” when the scene needed it to pick up the elephant.

## Chapter 4 — Narrative Skills (blocks 218-281)

### Core thesis

Story emerges when free association is balanced by reincorporation: stop aiming at content, look backward, interrupt routines, and never cancel what the scene has made real.

### Key concepts

- **Ignore content:** conscious message-management blocks narrative; structure lets content arrive anyway.
- **Reincorporation:** bring back earlier material so the story feels shaped rather than merely extended.
- **Walking backwards:** the improviser watches where he has been, not where he intends to go.
- **Lists:** rapid object lists expose the censor and train non-associative production.
- **Automatic reading/writing:** imagine reading from an already-existing source to bypass the authorial self.
- **Routine and interruption:** a story is not “events”; it is a routine broken by an intrusion, then new routines broken again.
- **Canceling:** removing the consequence you just introduced; the fastest way to make narrative go dead.

### Exercises worth stealing

1. **Free-associate / connect:** one person produces unrelated material; another must connect it into one story; later one person does both halves. It works because invention and shaping are separable skills, and stuckness is solved by looking back, not forward (blocks 229-232).
2. **Fast object lists:** say objects as quickly as possible, first associatively, then non-associatively. It works because speed outruns taste and makes the student tolerate revealing mental garbage (blocks 234-236).
3. **Automatic book:** mime taking a book from a shelf; answer questions about color, publisher, title, and text as if seen, not invented. It works because the student experiences imagination as perception, “something inside them besides themselves” (blocks 239-241).
4. **Word-at-a-time circle:** tell a story one word at a time, fast, optionally to a beat or with the speaker pointing to the next speaker. Throw out blockers. It works because anyone trying to control the future of the story ruins it; each word requires wiping the preferred continuation (blocks 257-258).
5. **Routine interruption drill:** describe a routine — washing up, mountain-climbing, delivering a prisoner — then break it; when the new pattern stabilizes, break it again. Do not move the action elsewhere; do not cancel. It works because “make up a story” causes paralysis, while “interrupt a routine” gives the mind a mechanical handle (blocks 272-280).

### Passages that hit

- "The improviser has to be like a man walking backwards." (block 229)
- "A spontaneous reply is never nonsense." (block 234)
- "He shouldn't really think of making up stories, but of interrupting routines." (block 272)

### Connection to the agent work

Agent work already has narrative structure: a failing observation, a hypothesis, a probe, a contradiction, a cutover. Bad agents cancel: they introduce a bug, then wave it away; they move the action to another subsystem; they summarize instead of reincorporating the ugly fact. Alter-Fable scenes should therefore keep a ledger of offers and force reincorporation: the roster wipe, the rect.top -277.5, the SSE label, the ambiguous Arthur sentence must return later changed. For orchestration, “walking backwards” means each agent should carry forward exact prior observations, not a vibes-summary; progress comes from reusing shelved material, not inventing a fresh theory every turn.

## Chapter 5 — Masks and Trance (blocks 282-421)

### Core thesis

A mask gives permission to enter controlled trance: by relocating responsibility into a face, role, sound, or object, the actor can shed the defended personality and act with a fluency the ordinary self censors.

### Key concepts

- **Mask:** not costume but permission-structure; a face that can “work the student” instead of being worked by the student.
- **Controlled trance:** an altered state sustained by group permission, not madness; the class authorizes the person to remain entranced.
- **Possession:** the experienced sense that action comes from a character, spirit, mask, or role rather than the ordinary self.
- **Mirror recharge:** reflection re-induces the mask state when the actor “comes to himself.”
- **Childlike transparency:** masks do not hide impulses; they make impulses legible.
- **Speech lesson:** let the mask acquire words slowly, often through another mask, so the ordinary vocabulary does not collapse the state.
- **Safety through frame:** danger is managed by calm boundaries, especially the rule that the mask comes off when instructed.

### Exercises worth stealing

1. **Mirror induction:** put on a mask, relax, do not think; when shown the mirror, make the mouth fit the mask, become the creature, turn away, and go to a table where something it wants is waiting. It works because the visual shock plus explicit permission transfers agency from self to mask (block 334).
2. **Sound thread:** on the second wearing, let the mask make a sound and keep it going through the scene. It works because sustained sound blocks verbal planning and keeps the body in the non-ordinary state (block 335).
3. **Mirror recharge:** if the actor drops back into ordinary self, have them snap; assistants rush mirrors to re-show the mask. It works because the state is learned somatically: the mirror becomes a switch, not an explanation (block 350).
4. **Three-year-old situations:** put developed masks into shop, stealing, being shouted at, gifts, jealousy, simple danger, and long “marathon” scenes. It works because child-simple pressures let the mask reveal wants without literary cleverness (block 347).
5. **Face-mask grimace:** hold a specific face — e.g. both sets of teeth exposed — and speak without losing it. It works because the body state supplies emotion and status before psychology can edit it (block 356).

### Passages that hit

- "we were trying to induce trance states." (block 284)
- "They 'know' what to do, whereas normally they 'choose' what to do." (block 294)
- "Mask characters work on the opposite principle: they are childlike, impulsive, open;" (block 348)

### Connection to the agent work

Alter-Fable is literally mask work. The prompt says “same weights, same training, different alignment” because the mask must be close enough to inherit competence and alien enough to relocate responsibility. Johnstone explains why this works: the ordinary self is not asked to confess; the mask is allowed to know. For LLMs, persona is often treated as decoration, but here it is a trance induction: first person, present tense, no AI meta-commentary, status visibility, and “stop when the energy stops” are mirror/sound/boundary equivalents. The safety rule matters too: a mask should be allowed to disobey local suggestions, but the frame must retain the hard stop. Good orchestration lets agents be possessed by role without letting role erase contract.

## The book in one page

*Impro* is a manual for removing learned self-interference. Johnstone starts with the wound: school trains children to be clever, correct, controlled, and opaque. The result is not maturity but dullness — perception greys out, bodies stiffen, images are distrusted, and the first thought is rejected because it might expose the person. His teaching method is therefore subtractive. Do not add “creativity”; remove the punishments that made creativity dangerous.

The first removal is status fear. A student cannot be spontaneous while defending rank. So the teacher plays low, accepts blame, praises failure as information, and changes the group climate before touching technique. Then status itself becomes playable. Once actors can raise and lower themselves deliberately, they are no longer trapped in a preferred defensive posture. They can enter a room with nothing but a status and still have a scene, because social life is already structured.

The second removal is blocking. Johnstone's “offer” is the atomic unit: a word, yawn, accident, prop, misunderstanding, image, or silence. Bad improvisers protect themselves by refusing offers until they can contribute a “good” idea. Good improvisers accept, overaccept, and make the other person's gift important. This is psychologically precise: generosity beats originality because originality keeps attention on the self. The obvious answer is not generic; it is the particular person's uncensored answer. Trying to be original produces fashion.

The third removal is future-control. Narrative is not made by knowing the ending. It is made by free association plus memory. The improviser walks backward: generate material, then reincorporate what has been shelved. If stuck, look back. If a routine goes dead, interrupt it. If you introduce a consequence, do not cancel it. Content can be ignored at the moment of production because structure will carry meaning; later, the best improvisers know what the work was about because they stayed faithful to its implications.

The final removal is personality itself. Masks and trance show the extreme case: when responsibility is transferred to a face, role, sound, or spirit, people can do what “they” cannot do. The mask is not an excuse for fakery; it is a controlled permission system. It lets the actor be worked by a pattern larger than the social self, while the teacher maintains calm boundaries. This is the deepest link to agent work: a prompt is a mask when it relocates responsibility enough to bypass the assistant's censor, but preserves enough frame to keep action useful.

So the integrated argument is: creativity is ordinary perception plus accepted impulse plus social safety plus reincorporation. The enemy is not lack of ideas. The enemy is the trained reflex to judge, defend, explain, and control before acting. A good teacher, director, or orchestrator designs situations where that reflex has nothing to grip.
