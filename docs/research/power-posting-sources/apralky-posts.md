## 1. FLAGSHIP — Automation → repression → the limits of redistribution (essay-tweet)
- **Date:** 2026-06-19 (21:43 UTC)
- **URL:** https://x.com/apralky/status/2068087201368265089
- **Theme:** AI / automation / capital vs. labour / acceleration. **Engagement:** ~153 likes, ~16.9K views. (Quote-tweets Daron Acemoglu's "Automation and Repression" MIT working paper.)

> This is a pretty nice model for thinking about why it's crucial not to take the labs at their word in their redistributive promises –
>
> The cost of redistributive policy scales with capital's income relative to labour's – in the model it's financed by a tax rate, and the tax rate itself is increasing as labour's share falls and capital's share increases. This is because redistribution is intended to function as a disincentive – if workers revolt, they give up their normal wage and the redistributed benefit, but if the revolt succeeds they stand to gain a payoff tied to capital returns. In this equation then the redistributed amount must make the expected payoff from revolting lower than the expected payoff from not revolting, so straightforwardly it needs to be a larger fraction of total output as the ratio of capital's share to labour's share grows.
>
> In simpler terms, the amount capital-owners (and the capitalist state acting on their behalf) have to pay you in UBI to stop you from rioting on the street has to scale with what you expect to gain on net by rioting on the street – which is equal to how much you expect your piece to be worth in case of a successful expropriation of capital, e.g. AI labs, weighted by the probability of success, less what you're already making as a wage (and less the redistributed benefit + the cost of revolt).
>
> The cost of repression, on the other hand, acts differently. Armies, drones, digital surveillance, etc. – which can be used as an alternative means to quell revolt – all have a ~fixed cost as a share of output. As AI gets better, these become both more potent and more accessible.
>
> The capital-owners who find themselves having to pay you a progressively higher UBI as they get relatively richer also find themselves having increasingly cheaper ways to suppress you by force.
>
> Then, the main reasons redistribution could still remain attractive to the increasingly rich capital-owners are:
>
> A) the cost of repression is set sufficiently high from the outset, such that it can't be outstripped by an increasingly expensive redistribution on a relevant timeline, or
> B) repression mechanisms are imperfect, such that the residual risk of revolt after attempted repression is meaningful, or
> C) The risk of revolt is very low, such that even in the case of repression being very cheap, redistribution remains cheaper, or
> D) The capital-owner (human or non-human) is a benevolent actor. (Outside the model)
>
> It sounds fair to say that A and B are very unlikely under fast-takeoff assumptions, absent targeted policy against capital-owners' interests –
>
> In the case of A, sufficiently advanced AI models are expected to make repression unboundedly trivial, and their labs are expected to become unboundedly wealthy. That repressing a likely human revolt will bear a meaningful enough cost on a post-AGI lab-owner that it favours paying out a higher tax rate would require a slow (or jagged) takeoff.
>
> B is impossible under unboundedly powerful models, a sufficiently powerful AI is expected to repress any human revolt with maximum certainty. Again, ending up in B is conditioned on models not approaching the "good enough to reliably repress human revolts" state anytime soon, a pretty slow-takeoff.
>
> C also seems unreliable – it's a broadly subscribed to narrative that pitchforks are coming, and the relevant human capitalists have certainly sounded conscious of this (though a host of frictions can still keep P(successful revolt) low).
>
> Which kind of implies that absent an effective policy backstop by a state acting explicitly independently of capital-owners' interests, with fast enough model development, and an angry/capable enough population, we're relying on D – the benevolence of capital-owners – to avoid dystopian outcomes.
>
> Note that policy in these cases would only act as a backstop if it's enforceable, i.e. the state must not only explicitly act independently of capital-owners' interests, but it must also remain more capable than the capital-owners/labs (which only seems doable if the state can out-steer sufficiently powerful models vs. the labs), or weaker but sufficiently powerful such that overruling enforcement poses a large enough added cost for the capital-owners/labs onto repression to equalize it to redistribution – though the latter case is unsustainable for the same reasons as above (as models get better, that relative cost pales vs. a tax rate too, just like the cost of repression does).
>
> Also note that under this setup, policy working as a backstop now is no guarantee that it will continue to work as a backstop later – and in the same light, that the state/labs might show redistributive tendencies now is not meaningfully informative of their benevolence, i.e. it's not an indicator that they will continue to show redistributive tendencies later, when the relative cost of redistribution rises.
>
> Clearly the scenarios I've used above, like those involving unboundedly powerful models, come with a bunch of "we'll have bigger things to worry about" baggage, but still I think they all hold their role in the intermediate stages.

## 4. AI models give risk-averse, non-Machiavellian, negative-EV life advice
- **Date:** 2026-06-18 (21:45 UTC)
- **URL:** https://x.com/apralky/status/2067725465511731705
- **Theme:** LLMs / alignment side-effects / rationalist. **Engagement:** ~332 likes, ~15.8K views.

> A pattern I can't help but notice in speaking with LLMs about general life advice is that they seem to be a lot more risk averse than I am. I think the average person probably has a more concave utility function than I do, and maybe this is a case of my utility function being an outlier and not the LLM's. This would be in line with the existing studies on this, which are inconclusive and overall probably show a roughly human-like risk aversion.
>
> Still, the fact that my AI is noticeably more risk averse than I am (I've had moments of being recommended extreme compliance/caution in instances where, intuitively, I'd only expect something like 95th+ percentile risk-averse humans to be compliant/cautious, for example advising against posting something mildly provocative) makes me want to defer to it a lot less, and I suspect that at this stage it's given me on net negative-EV life advice cumulatively.
>
> Overall it would be intuitive for this drift to continue, given that the incentive for a higher "risk aversion parameter" in post-training should be higher than the incentive for a higher risk aversion parameter when consuming the AI's output – edge cases of extreme harm as a result of the AI's advice in say 0.0…1% of consumers can cause meaningful regulatory damage to the AI lab, and it's probably fair to say that advice-giving with the normal human utility function would clearly lead to extreme harm in at least 0.0…1% of consumers.
>
> In the same light, I've noticed that AIs are meaningfully less Machiavellian in giving life advice than would be expected of an average person (if both were answering honestly), and certainly less Machiavellian than would be expected of an extremely successful person. This also often makes their life advice seem negative-EV – e.g. the model refuses to suggest one manipulates somebody for personal gain even if it might be optimal – these are more explicitly pursued in post-training than the risk parameters.
>
> These run somewhat contrary to the common wisdom that we'll gradually converge to a world in which an increasingly large number of people either outsource their life decisions to AIs, or fall behind in life outcomes? Could we not see a different drift in the near term – where people not making their own decisions become suboptimally risk-averse, suboptimally prosocial, etc. and the opposite gets rewarded?
